Technical Report Requirements and Writing Guide

ECE Emerge — EEC1 Spring 2026

Read this before writing the Phase 1 Milestone Report.

The Phase 1 Milestone Report is a structured practice run for the full technical
report due at the end of the project. The section structure maps directly onto a
full engineering report. The expectations — precision, justification, team process
— are the same. Getting these habits right in Phase 1 makes the final report
significantly easier to write and significantly better.

Why Technical Reports Matter

A technical report is not a record of what you did. It is a document that allows
a qualified engineer who was not present to:

  • Understand what problem was being solved and why the design approach was chosen
  • Verify the results independently from your raw data
  • Identify limitations or discrepancies and assess whether they affect the design
  • Decide whether the design is ready to proceed to the next phase

Your audience is a competent ECE engineer, not your lab partner. Do not explain
Ohm's law. Do explain why you chose $G_\text{INA} = 500$ instead of $G_\text{INA} = 8000$.

A full technical report also requires an Abstract and Introduction, which
are not required for Phase 1 but will be required for the final report. Guidance
for those sections is included at the end of this document.

What Belongs in Each Section

Section 1 — Design Decisions (Design)

Purpose: Explain what was designed and, critically, why.

This section answers the question: "What engineering choices did the team make,
and what was the reasoning behind each choice?"

Must include:

  • The chosen value and the calculation that produced it
  • The justification for the choice — why this value and not another
  • The standard component actually used, its value, and the resulting performance

Does NOT include:

  • A description of how the breadboard was wired
  • A repetition of the lab instructions or the design procedure

Weak:

"We chose $R_g = 120\,\Omega$ for the INA125."

Strong:

"A total gain of 10,000 is required to map the 0.5 mV bridge output at 500 g
to the full 5 V ADC window. Concentrating this gain in the INA125 alone would
require $R_g = 6\,\Omega$, where contact resistance is comparable to $R_g$
itself and would introduce significant gain error. Distributing gain as
$G_\text{INA} = 500$ and $G_\text{sum} = 20$ raises $R_g$ to $120\,\Omega$,
well above the $30\,\Omega$ practical lower bound. The nearest standard 1%
value, $121\,\Omega$, produces $G_\text{INA} = 500.8$, a 0.16% deviation
from target."

The difference: the weak version states a fact. The strong version demonstrates
engineering judgment — the author considered an alternative, identified its failure
mode, and justified the chosen approach quantitatively.

Section 2 — Bring-Up Results (Results)

Purpose: Report what the instruments measured. Numbers, units, photographs.

This section answers: "What did the measurements show?"

Must include:

  • Every required measurement with its numerical value and unit
  • Percent error from the designed value where applicable
  • Photographs with figure numbers and captions (see Style section below)
  • Organized presentation — a table is almost always clearer than prose for
    multiple measurements

Does NOT include:

  • Explanations of why numbers differ from design (that is Discussion)
  • Descriptions of the procedure for taking the measurement

Weak:

"The summing amplifier output at zero load was close to $+2.5$ V."

Strong:

"At 0 V INA input, the summing amplifier output measured $+2.47$ V, a 1.2%
error from the designed $+2.50$ V. At the full-scale input of $+1.00$ V, the
output measured $-2.51$ V, a 0.4% error from $-2.50$ V. The measured transfer
function slope is $-4.98$ V/V, compared to the designed $-5.00$ V/V."

Numbers in context, with units, with comparisons to design — that is a Results
section.

Section 3 — Discrepancy Analysis (Discussion)

Purpose: Explain what the results mean, especially where they depart from design.

This section answers: "Why did the measurements differ from design, and does it matter?"

Must include:

  • For every measurement that deviated more than 10% from the design value:
    a specific proposed cause
  • A statement of whether the discrepancy affects the system's ability to meet
    specifications
  • The measurement that provided the highest confidence in the design, and why

Does NOT include:

  • New results (those belong in Section 2)
  • Vague attributions like "human error" without identifying a specific mechanism

Weak:

"The gain was slightly off because of resistor tolerances."

Strong:

"The measured INA gain of 487 V/V is 2.6% below the designed 500 V/V. The
most likely cause is the combined effect of the 1% resistor tolerance on the
$121\,\Omega$ gain resistor and the $\pm20$ ppm/°C temperature coefficient
over the lab temperature range. This deviation shifts the full-scale ADC
output by approximately 107 counts out of 4096 — within the correction range
of the MATLAB calibration routine, so the specification is still met."

The key move: state the deviation, propose a specific physical cause, and
evaluate whether it matters for the system.

Section 4 — Plan for Phase 2 (Conclusion / Future Work)

Purpose: State what comes next and what must change before proceeding.

Must include:

  • Specific Phase 2 objectives (MATLAB interface, calibration routine, GUI)
  • Any design changes identified during bring-up that must be incorporated
    before Phase 2 begins
  • If no design changes are needed, state that explicitly and identify the
    measurement that confirms it

A conclusion is not a summary of what was already said. It looks forward.

Abstract and Introduction (Required for Final Report, Not Phase 1)

Abstract

The abstract is a self-contained summary of the entire report: what was done,
the key numerical results, and the key conclusion. It is written last and is
typically 100–150 words.

It does NOT preview the report ("Section 3 discusses..."). It reports the
outcome directly.

Template structure:
1. One sentence on what was built and its purpose
2. One to two sentences on the key design parameters or approach
3. One to two sentences on the key measured results (numbers)
4. One sentence on the conclusion or readiness for next phase

Introduction

The introduction establishes context and states the objective. It answers:
"What is the system, what does it need to do, and what does this report cover?"

It does NOT describe results or design decisions — those belong in later sections.
Typically 150–250 words for a lab-scale report.

Team Writing Process

Teams that skip these steps produce reports that read as four separate documents
stapled together.

Step 1 — Meet before anyone writes (30 minutes)

As a team, reach agreement on:

  • What the key results were, stated as numbers
  • What the most significant discrepancy was and why it happened
  • Which design decisions most need justification (not just statement)

This is not about assigning paragraphs. It is about establishing a shared
narrative before writing begins. If the team does not agree on what happened
before writing, the report will contradict itself.

Step 2 — Assign sections

Assign one team member as primary author for each section. Sections are written
independently. Every member contributes.

Step 3 — Assemble and read aloud

One member assembles all sections into the complete document. The team then reads
the full draft aloud — one person reads while the others follow. Do not skip
this step.

Reading aloud forces every sentence to be heard, not just skimmed. This catches:

  • Sections that contradict each other on the same numbers
  • Design decisions stated in Section 1 that are not consistent with Section 2
    results
  • Sentences that were clear to the author but are ambiguous when spoken
  • Inconsistent notation, units, or figure references

Step 4 — Revise and submit

Only after the read-aloud pass is the document ready to submit. One member
submits on behalf of the team; all member names appear on the title page.

Style Requirements

Units

Every number requires a unit. No exceptions.

Units
Wrong Right
The gain was 487. The gain was 487 V/V.
The resistance was 121. The resistance was $121\,\Omega$.
The output was 2.47. The output voltage was $+2.47$ V.

Figures and photographs

Every figure (photograph, schematic, plot, oscilloscope screenshot) must have:

  1. A figure number: Figure 1, Figure 2, etc.
  2. A caption that states what is shown and what the key observation is
  3. A reference in the body text: "...as shown in Figure 3."

A photograph without a caption is decoration, not evidence.

Weak caption: Figure 2: Breadboard.

Strong caption: Figure 2: Completed Stage 2 breadboard. The summing amplifier
(LMC662, center) is tested in isolation with a DC input from the M2K power supply.
Decoupling capacitors are visible at the supply pins.

Tense and person

  • Past tense for actions performed: "The gain resistor was measured at $119\,\Omega$."
  • Present tense for general principles: "The INA125 gain formula is
    $G = 4 + 60\,\text{k}\Omega / R_g$."
  • Third person throughout: not "we measured" — "the gain was measured"; not
    "I chose" — "the gain was selected."

Significant figures

Report measurements to the precision of the instrument. The M2K voltmeter resolves
to approximately 1 mV; report voltages to 1 mV, not 1 µV. Reporting
$V_\text{out} = 2.4700013$ V implies a precision the instrument does not have.

Equations and symbols

Define every symbol the first time it appears. Use consistent notation throughout:
if Section 1 calls the gain resistor $R_g$, Sections 2 and 3 must also call it $R_g$.

What a Technical Report Is Not

What a Technical Report Is Not
This is NOT a technical report This IS a technical report
A restatement of the lab instructions A record of engineering decisions and measured outcomes
A lab notebook ("first we did X, then we did Y") A structured document: Design, Results, Discussion
A list of raw numbers Numbers in context, with units, comparisons, and interpretation
Separate sections with different voices and formats A consistent document that reads as if one author wrote it
"The results were good" "The measured gain of 487 V/V is within 2.6% of the 500 V/V design target"
"Error was due to human error" "The 2.6% gain error is consistent with the 1% tolerance of the gain resistor"

Submission Checklist

Complete this before submitting.

  • [ ] All team member names appear on the title page
  • [ ] Every number has a unit
  • [ ] Every figure has a number, a descriptive caption, and a reference in the text
  • [ ] Section 1 (Design) explains why each choice was made, not just what was chosen
  • [ ] Section 2 (Results) contains measured values only — no interpretation
  • [ ] Section 3 (Discussion) proposes a specific physical cause for every deviation > 10%
  • [ ] Section 3 states whether each discrepancy affects the ability to meet specifications
  • [ ] Section 4 (Plan for Phase 2) identifies any required design changes before Phase 2
  • [ ] The complete draft was read aloud by the team before submission
  • [ ] Notation and units are consistent across all sections
